The list

Γεια σας, καλημέρα, καλησπέρα, καληνύχτα, ευχαριστώ, παρακαλώ, ναι, όχι, συγγνώμη, ορίστε. Ten words, one breath each, and between them they cover the entry, the yes, the no, the thanks, the squeeze past and the exit of an ordinary shop visit.

Γεια σας. — YA sas — hello, and goodbye, with the same word
Ευχαριστώ, γεια σας. — ef-kha-ree-STO, YA sas — the exit

Say γεια σας and never choose

Greek has the two-way split most European languages have: γεια σου to one person you are close to, γεια σας to anyone else and to more than one person. You do not have to work out which is which at the door. Use γεια σας every time — with the shopkeeper, with the waiter, with the person on the ferry — and it is correct in all of them. When γεια σου comes back at you, that is warmth arriving, not a correction.

Παρακαλώ does three jobs

It is please on the end of a request, you're welcome after a thank you, and go ahead when someone is waving you forward or picking up the phone. You will hear it far more often than you say it, and in every one of those positions it is the same four syllables with the beat at the end.

Ναι, παρακαλώ. — NE, pa-ra-ka-LO — yes, please
Ευχαριστώ. — Παρακαλώ. — thank you — you're welcome

The midday gap is real, and nobody has fixed it

There is no normative source setting the hour where καλημέρα gives way to καλησπέρα. Three usages coexist, and there is a named prescriptive position — Babiniotis holds that the evening starts at sunset and that καλησπέρα at noon is a foreign import. Meanwhile καλό μεσημέρι exists precisely to cover the ambiguous stretch. The honest instruction is the useful one: in the early afternoon, listen for the greeting that reaches you and return it.

Two politeness systems, not two temperaments

Work by Sifianou and others describes Greek as oriented to positive politeness — involvement and closeness — and English to negative politeness — distance and non-imposition. It is a description of how the two systems build a polite move, nothing more. The predictable consequence is symmetrical: a directly worded offer or question can read as intrusive to an English ear, and English hedging can read as cool to a Greek one. Neither reading is about anybody's character.

About English here

Greece places high in the commercial proficiency indices, with speaking as its weakest sub-score. Those indices are built from online, self-selected samples, so they are evidence of a trend and never a count of speakers. Practically: expect English at hotel desks, ferry offices and city centres, expect less of it at a village bakery, and do not build a plan on either. The short Greek sentence keeps its value exactly where the sub-score is lowest.
