The four words are custom, not law

Greek food-safety law does not know what a taverna is. It files taverna, estiatório, ouzerí and psistariá together as επιχειρήσεις μαζικής εστίασης under the notification regime of law 4442/2016. The four words are a commercial and customary distinction, and that is exactly what makes them useful: they tell you what to expect on the menu and what register the room is in, and they promise nothing beyond that. A tsipourádiko is mostly a Vólos and Thessaly thing, so treat that one as regional rather than national.

Τι έχετε σήμερα; — What have you got today?

Magireftá and tis óras

Magireftá are the dishes cooked earlier in trays and pots — stuffed vegetables, beans, briám, things that sat and got better. Tis óras is what goes on the grill when you order it. Asking éhete magireftá names a whole family in two words, and it is the family a laminated tourist menu tends to bury.

Έχετε μαγειρευτά; — Do you have pot dishes?
Θα πάρω αυτό. — I'll have that one.

Bread, water and the thing that cannot be charged

Cover charge is prohibited outright, and no product at all — bread and bottled water explicitly included — may be served and charged without prior consent, at €500 an item. Bread can perfectly well appear on the bill if it was offered and you said yes. So the question to listen for is thélete, and the useful answer is whichever one you actually mean.

Ordering for the table

Mezédes are small plates that come to the middle of the table, so ordering a few of them settles what everybody eats in one sentence instead of four. That is a fact about how the plates arrive, and it is the whole of the claim this course makes. Separately, and it proves nothing about your table: UNESCO's inscription of the Mediterranean Diet, which Greece helped submit, records that eating together is foundational to the identity and continuity of communities across the Mediterranean. That is heritage listing, not an anthropological rule about who shares what — do not read it as one.

Mporó na do

You will read in a lot of travel writing that visitors go into Greek kitchens to look in the pots. This course does not make that claim: there is no source behind it. What the course gives you is the sentence — mporó na do, may I have a look — which is ordinary, polite, and answerable with a yes or a no by whoever is standing there.
