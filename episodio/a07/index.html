Two politeness systems, and a symmetrical misreading

This is one of the few things in the course with an academic literature behind it. Politeness research places Greek on the positive-politeness side — involvement, closeness, the assumption that people want to be included — and English on the negative-politeness side, which is built on distance and not imposing. The consequence is documented and it runs both ways: the English speaker experiences Greek warmth as intrusive or abrupt, and the Greek speaker experiences English reserve as cold. Neither reading is about you personally, and knowing that the mirror exists is most of the fix.

Εσείς, εσύ, and why this is a signal and never a decision

Greek has the standard T/V pair: γεια σας and γεια σου, είστε and είσαι, σας λένε and σε λένε. It is tempting to treat this as something to master, and this course deliberately does not. Choosing between them is a reading of the social situation made under pressure, and it changes the verb as well — which makes it exactly the kind of thing a visitor gets wrong at the worst moment. So it stays receptive. Your rule is one line long: start on εσείς with everyone, and move only when somebody moves you. The move is usually explicit — πάμε στον ενικό, or simply the other person switching mid-sentence — and the correct response is to switch too, immediately and without commentary. Declining, or carefully staying formal after being invited, is the one way to get this wrong.

The no, the volume and the palm

Three things an English ear misreads by default. The no is a short upward tilt of the head, usually with a small click of the tongue, and it is complete — no sentence follows because none is needed. It reads as impatience to a visitor and it is closer to a shrug. Volume is the second: a loud table is normally an engaged table, and an exchange that sounds like a row is very often about a football result. Look at the faces rather than the decibels. The third runs the other way, and it is the one signal on this list you can send by accident: an open palm pushed towards someone's face is a recognised insult here. Wave with your fingers together, or raise a hand rather than pushing it forward.

Leaving is a process, not an event

Να πηγαίνω — I should get going — opens the goodbye. It does not close it. Expect μη βιάζεσαι, expect one more coffee to be poured, expect to be walked to the door and possibly to the corner. Announcing your departure ten minutes before you want to leave is not indecision; it is the correct amount of runway. Καλή συνέχεια is what you say to someone whose day is continuing without you, στο καλό is the warm send-off you will hear back, and χάρηκα πολύ closes a first meeting. None of them is a request to leave — they are all release.

The receptive rule, one more time

You will hear εσύ used about you, around you and at you long before you have any business producing it. That is fine. Receptive knowledge is what this module trades in: recognising the signal costs you nothing and being wrong about producing it costs a small amount of warmth at exactly the moment you were building some. Wait to be moved.
