What the index measures, and what it does not

The EF English Proficiency Index 2025 puts Greece at 592, twentieth globally, in the band it calls High, ten points down on the previous edition. EF states the limitation itself: respondents are self-selected, they need internet access, and they skew towards people already interested in languages. So the index does not measure the English of the Greek population — it measures the English of Greeks who decided to sit an online test. That is worth saying plainly, because the number gets quoted as though it were a census. This course will tell you that Greece figures among the high-proficiency countries in commercial self-selected indices. It will not tell you what proportion of people you meet will speak English, because no material in this research supports such a figure.

Συγγνώμη, μιλάτε αγγλικά; — Excuse me, do you speak English?
Ελληνικά, λίγα μόνο. — Greek — only a little.

The asymmetry inside the number, which is the actionable part

The skill breakdown is where the practical instruction lives: reading 600, listening 592, writing 571, speaking 533. Speaking is the weakest of the four by a clear margin. Translated into a counter, that means the person in front of you may follow your English comfortably and still find answering it hard work — and it means the gap between what they understand and what they will volunteer is wider than you would guess from the first exchange. Two habits follow. Shorten your sentences and slow the delivery rather than raising the volume. And when the exchange contains a number, a time, a platform or a street, move it onto paper: μπορείτε να το γράψετε; costs four words and removes the skill that scores lowest from the problem entirely.

The six words

Καλημέρα, καλησπέρα, γεια σας, παρακαλώ, ευχαριστώ, συγγνώμη. That is the entire courtesy kit and it covers nearly every counter you will stand at. Two of them do more than one job. Παρακαλώ is please, you're welcome and go ahead all at once, so you will hear it answering your thanks as often as you say it asking for something, and as a question it means say that again. Γεια σας is the polite form to a stranger or to more than one person, γεια σου the familiar one, and if you are not sure which, the plural is never wrong. What none of this comes with is a promised effect: nobody has studied what a well-pronounced ευχαριστώ actually buys, and this course does not claim a number for it. What can be said is smaller and truer — the words are short, you will hear all six back at you within a day, and using them puts you inside the exchange rather than beside it.

Midday, and the greeting nobody agrees about

The switch from καλημέρα to καλησπέρα has no normative source. There is a well-known prescriptive position — the lexicographer Babiniotis argues that the εσπέρα begins at sunset and that καλησπέρα at noon is imitation of a foreign habit — and there is ordinary practice, in which three different usages coexist quite happily. Between about noon and five you will hear natives hesitate, correct themselves and laugh about it, and καλό μεσημέρι exists precisely for that stretch. The honest teaching is that the ambiguity is real and shared. You cannot get this wrong in a way anybody notices, because the person opposite is not sure either.

The Latin-letter version is a different language of the same word

The romanisation on road signs is a standard — ΕΛΟΤ 743, second edition 2001, identical to ISO 843:1997, adopted by the PCGN in 1996 and recommended by the UN in 1987 — and the PCGN's own Greece factfile of April 2020 records both that it is very widely used on Greek road signs and printed guides, and that it is a less reliable guide to Modern Greek pronunciation than the older system it replaced. That is the whole warning: the Latin spelling is built to be converted back into Greek letters, not to be read aloud by an English speaker. Use it to match a word on a sign to a word on your ticket. For the sound, ask a person — πώς το λένε αυτό στα ελληνικά; — and copy the stress off the voice, because the accent mark on a Greek word of two syllables or more tells you exactly where it goes.
