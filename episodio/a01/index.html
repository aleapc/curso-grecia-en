Ναι is yes, and it is the trap

The single most expensive mishearing available to an English speaker in Greece is ναι, which means yes and arrives sounding like nah. Όχι is no, and it is the longer, heavier word of the two. Once you have this the right way round, a great many exchanges stop looking like refusals: the short light word was agreement, and the person who said it is already doing what you asked.

Ναι, ναι, εντάξει. — Yes, yes, fine.
Όχι, όχι σήμερα. — No, not today.

Εντάξει, λοιπόν, καλά, πάμε, ορίστε

Εντάξει is fine, agreed, no need, we are done, and it turns up several times a minute. Λοιπόν opens a decision and is the best thing your ear can be tuned for, because the useful half of the sentence follows it. Καλά is roughly all right and often signals a closing. Πάμε means we are going, now. Ορίστε does two opposite jobs — here you are, and sorry, say that again — separated only by whether the voice rises at the end.

What disappears at speed

Δεν loses its ν, so δεν ξέρω comes out as one short run of sound rather than two words. Ρε is an address particle between people who know each other, and it appears constantly in ordinary talk; recognise it as warmth and never use it yourself, because in a visitor's mouth it reads as an impression of Greek rather than as Greek. Έλα means come, and is also the standard hello on the phone, and also go on, out with it — three uses of one word, sorted entirely by context.

The sign is not a pronunciation guide

Road signs are romanised with ELOT 743, second edition 2001, the same as ISO 843:1997, adopted by the British PCGN in 1996 and recommended by the UN in 1987. The PCGN's own Greece Toponymic Factfile of April 2020 says it is widely used on road signs and in printed guidebooks, that place names online are not romanised consistently, and that ELOT 743 is a less reliable guide to Modern Greek pronunciation than the older system it replaced. Practical consequence: use the romanised form to match a name on a ticket, a map or a departure board, and use your ear — not the spelling — for how it sounds.

English, unevenly

Greece scores 592 in the EF English Proficiency Index 2025, twentieth globally and in the High band, with speaking at 533 as its weakest skill. EF states plainly that respondents are self-selected and need internet access, so the number describes people who chose to take an online test, not the population. Read it as: English is often available, especially at counters that serve travellers, and a short Greek sentence keeps its value exactly where speaking scores lowest.
