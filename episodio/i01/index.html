Why this part has no hour in it

There is a European time-use survey with exactly the table you would want, and Greece takes part in it, but the Greek figures were not extracted for this course. So the course does not print an hour. What it gives you instead is the question, which works in every town and does not go out of date: ti óra tróte edó.

Τι ώρα τρώτε εδώ; — What time do you eat here?
Είναι νωρίς ακόμα; — Is it still early?

The kitchen and the room are two different questions

I kouzína is the cooking; the room around it often stays open for a drink long after the pots are off. Asking ti óra anígi i kouzína gets you the hour that matters, and méhri ti óra servírete gets you the other end of it.

Μέχρι τι ώρα σερβίρετε; — Until what time do you serve?

Kaliméra, kalispéra and the gap in between

There is no rule fixing the moment one becomes the other. Three usages live side by side, and there is a named prescriptive position holding that the evening starts at sunset and that kalispéra at midday is imported. Greek also has kaló mesiméri for exactly the ambiguous stretch. The honest teaching is that natives hesitate there too — so take back whatever comes at you and stop auditing yourself.

Sunday, and the exception older than the rule

Sunday closing was instituted in 1909 and it already carried the exception for restaurants, tavernas, patisseries, cafés and tourist shops. The current framework is article 16 of law 4177/2013, with declared exceptions and ministerially designated tourist zones. The shop side of that framework is not settled — a liberalisation was struck down as unconstitutional and the question is still argued about locally — so treat it as something to check on the day rather than as a fixed timetable. The part that has not moved in a century is the part you eat in.
