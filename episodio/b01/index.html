The inversion nobody tells you about

Theta and delta — the th of thin and the th of this — are among the rarest consonants in the world's languages, and most learners of English spend years on them. You were born into them. Greek hands you both back for free, which means the phonetic bill for this trip is far smaller than it looks from the outside.

Αθήνα — a-THEE-na — the th of thin
Δεν καταλαβαίνω. — dhen ka-ta-la-VE-no — the th of this

Chi is the only genuinely new sound

Chi is made where a k is made, but the tongue never quite closes, so air keeps flowing. Before a, o and ou it is the throaty version; before e and i it thins out towards the h of huge. English speakers reach for a k and the word arrives as a different one — cheri and keri are not the same. The hand cue is a flat palm pushed slowly forward: as long as the palm is moving, the air is still running.

χάρτης / κάρτα — KHAR-tees / KAR-ta — a map / a card
Χαίρετε. — KHE-re-te — hello there

The beat is printed for you

Greek writes the stress on nearly every word of two syllables or more. That mark is not decoration and it is not optional: it tells you exactly where the flick goes. English speakers default to their own stress habits and the word lands somewhere else. The whole point of the hand in this part is that it makes the disagreement visible — if your flick and the printed mark are in different places, you can see it before you hear it.

Two letters the eye has to unlearn

Rho is written like a Latin P and is an r; eta is written like a Latin H and is an i. Those two account for most first-week misreadings — ΡΟΔΟΣ read as Podos is the standard example. Add nu, which looks like a v and is an n, and chi, which looks like an x and is not. Beta is a v, xi is the x, and psi is ps. The rest of the alphabet is more helpful than it looks.

Signomi, and why it is here in part one

The double gamma before nu comes out as ng followed by n — the same joint that sits in the middle of wing nut. Build it in three pieces, sing–no–me, then close the gaps. It is the hardest word in the starter kit and it is also the one that opens every repair you will ever need, which is why it gets drilled as a sound here, two parts before it becomes an opener.

The sign is not the pronunciation

The Latin lettering beside the Greek on road signs follows a national standard. That standard was chosen so the spelling can be turned back into Greek letters, not so it can be read aloud, and the British naming authority that adopted it says plainly it is a less reliable guide to pronunciation than the older system it replaced. Use it to match a place name on a map. Do not use it to say the name out loud.
