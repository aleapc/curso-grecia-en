The first answer is never the country

Από πού είστε gets you a village, an island, a region or a neighbourhood far more often than it gets you Greece. That is not evasion and it is not a political statement; it is simply the unit people carry. The follow-up από ποιο μέρος is three words long and it is the single highest-yield question in this module, because it moves the conversation from a country you have read about to a place the person in front of you can actually describe. Everything else in this part depends on that move.

Από πού είστε; — Where are you from?
Από ποιο μέρος; — Which part?

Eating together, and what that does and does not mean

The Mediterranean Diet sits on the UNESCO intangible heritage list — file ICH 00884, Greece among the submitting states — and the inscription puts commensality at the centre: eating together is described as the foundation of cultural identity and continuity across the basin. Read that for what it says. It is a statement about the shared meal across a whole region, not a rule that nobody in Greece ever orders a plate for themselves. In practice what you meet is μεζέδες and collective ordering: several small things put in the middle, chosen by whoever knows the kitchen, and eaten by everyone. Διαλέξτε εσείς hands that job to the person best placed to do it, and it is received as a compliment rather than as helplessness.

Taverna is a promise, not a licence

Ταβέρνα, εστιατόριο, ουζερί and ψησταριά are not legal categories at all: Greek law groups them together as mass-catering businesses under the notification regime of law 4442/2016. The distinction is commercial and customary — an expectation about the menu, the price and the register of the room — which is exactly why someone will correct you about it with feeling. When a woman tells you her place is a taverna and not a restaurant, she is telling you what she cooks and how loudly you may talk, not quoting a statute. Note also that the usual glosses vary by region; τσιπουράδικο, for instance, is above all a Volos and Thessaly word.

The closeness is measured, and so is your reading of it

Comparative work on politeness in Greek and English — Sifianou's book-length study and the volume she co-edited for John Benjamins — describes Greek as oriented towards positive politeness, built on involvement and closeness, and English as oriented towards negative politeness, built on distance and not imposing. The consequence is symmetrical and it is the most useful thing in this part: the English speaker can read the Greek exchange as abrupt or intrusive, and the Greek speaker can read the English one as cold or standoffish. Neither reading is accurate. When three personal questions arrive in the first two minutes, nothing has gone wrong; you are being included, in the ordinary way.

The past is not the small talk

You arrive carrying a very old idea of the place, and everybody knows you do. The ruins are genuinely enjoyed and genuinely lived beside, and the fastest way to be interesting is to ask about the century you are both standing in: what it is like here in winter, what this town has that the next one does not, what has changed in the last few years. The antiquity comes back into the conversation on its own, usually within ten minutes, and it comes back better because somebody chose to bring it.
