Order for the table

Drinks arrive small and food arrives in the middle. Nobody is guarding a plate, and the plate you were given is not a portion assigned to you. Ordering two or three things and pushing them into the centre is the ordinary move, and it is also the cheapest way to try five things instead of committing to one.

Τι έχετε ντόπιο; — What do you have that's local?
Άλλο ένα, παρακαλώ. — Another one, please.

The bread question, and why it is a question

A cover charge is prohibited outright, and no item may be served and billed without prior consent — bread and bottled water named explicitly, with a fine of €500 per item. Bread can perfectly well be charged if it was offered and you took it. So the useful sentence is not a complaint, it is a question asked in three words before the basket is opened.

The list on the wall

Prices have to be listed in Greek, visible at the entrance and again before you order, VAT included. A missing list carries a €1,000 fine and an item billed above the listed price carries €500. You do not need to argue with anything: reading the board on the way in tells you what the evening costs before you sit down.

Κερνάω εγώ

One person pays for the table. Trying to divide the bill mid-evening tends to be waved away, not because anyone is being extravagant but because the round is the unit and the next one is somebody else's. Saying κερνάω εγώ early is a gesture; producing a calculator at one in the morning is arithmetic on the pavement.

Tipping without a number

Tips are recognised in Greek tax law — up to €300 a month per worker is exempt from income tax — which tells you they exist and are normal. It tells you nothing about how much. There is no defensible national percentage, and rounding the bill up or leaving the coins is a complete answer everywhere.
